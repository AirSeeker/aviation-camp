## Introduction

Energy management is the process of planning, monitoring, and controlling altitude and airspeed targets in relation to the airplane's energy state. Rather than viewing pitch and power as isolated inputs, an energy-centered approach treats the aircraft as an integrated energy system.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p88_1.png" alt="Overview of energy management concepts in flight" />

By mastering energy management, pilots can:
1. Attain and maintain desired vertical flightpath and airspeed profiles (such as a stabilized climb or constant-speed descent).
2. Detect, correct, and prevent unintentional altitude and airspeed deviations.
3. Prevent an irreversible deceleration or excessive sink rate that could culminate in an accident.

> **Attention:** Mismanagement of mechanical energy (altitude and airspeed) is a primary contributing factor in aviation's three most common fatal accident categories: Loss of Control In-Flight (LOC-I), Controlled Flight Into Terrain (CFIT), and approach-and-landing accidents.

## Core Concepts

### The Airplane as an Open Energy System

In flight, an airplane functions as an **open energy system**. It continuously takes in energy from engine **Thrust** (derived from chemical energy in the fuel) and expends energy to overcome aerodynamic **Drag**. 

The total mechanical energy possessed by an airplane is the sum of its **potential energy** and **kinetic energy**:

$$\text{Total Mechanical Energy} = mgh + \frac{1}{2}mV^2$$

Where:
* $m$ = mass (aircraft **Weight**)
* $g$ = acceleration due to gravity
* $h$ = height (altitude)
* $V$ = velocity (**Airspeed**)

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p89_2.png" alt="Potential versus kinetic energy distribution in an aircraft" />

* **Potential Energy ($mgh$):** Stored in the form of altitude.
* **Kinetic Energy ($\frac{1}{2}mV^2$):** Stored in the form of airspeed.

Because energy can be transferred between these two states or added/removed from the system, the pilot acts as the energy manager.

---

### Frame of Reference for Managing Energy

A pilot’s operational frame of reference for managing the airplane's energy state is **airplane-centric**, relying on:
* **Indicated Altitude:** Displayed on the altimeter relative to Mean Sea Level (MSL), not height Above Ground Level (AGL).
* **Indicated Airspeed (IAS):** Displayed on the airspeed indicator relative to the surrounding airmass, not **Groundspeed**.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p90_3.png" alt="Frame of reference comparing indicated flight parameters to external ground references" />

Direct control inputs (elevator, throttle, and flight controls) directly govern Indicated Airspeed and Indicated Altitude. In contrast, external conditions such as rising terrain or wind shifts affect ground clearance and groundspeed. Pilots must anticipate external conditions and manipulate the energy state to maintain safe margins above terrain and clear of obstacles.

> **Attention:** Always monitor Indicated Airspeed (IAS) to evaluate the aircraft's aerodynamic energy state. Groundspeed does not determine stall margin or lift production.

## Net Energy Flow and the Energy Balance

The airplane continuously gains energy from thrust ($T$) and loses energy through drag ($D$). The difference between energy acquired and energy dissipated is the **net energy change** ($T - D$).

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p91_4.png" alt="Thrust versus drag and the net energy balance" />

The rate of energy change is referred to as **Specific Excess Power ($P_S$)**:

$$P_S = \frac{(T - D)V}{W}$$

Where:
* $T$ = Thrust
* $D$ = Drag
* $V$ = Velocity (airspeed)
* $W$ = Aircraft Weight

```
  Energy Gained (Thrust)  -  Energy Lost (Drag)  =  Net Energy Change
  [ Controlled by Throttle & Clean Airframe ]         [ Stored as Altitude or Airspeed ]
```

## Practical Application: Energy Transactions

Every flight maneuver involves an energy transaction. The pilot chooses whether to increase total energy, decrease it, maintain it, or trade potential and kinetic energy back and forth.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p92_5.png" alt="Energy transaction matrix showing climbs, descents, accelerations, and decelerations" />

### 1. Surplus Energy State ($T - D > 0$)
When engine thrust exceeds drag, total mechanical energy increases:
* **Climb at Constant Airspeed:** Surplus energy is channeled into potential energy (altitude).
* **Acceleration at Level Altitude:** Surplus energy is channeled into kinetic energy (airspeed).

### 2. Deficit Energy State ($T - D < 0$)
When drag exceeds thrust (e.g., reduced throttle, extended flaps, or high **Angle of Attack**):
* **Descent at Constant Airspeed:** Altitude is sacrificed to make up for the energy deficit.
* **Deceleration at Level Altitude:** Airspeed is sacrificed while maintaining height.

### 3. Balanced Energy State ($T - D = 0$)
When thrust exactly balances drag, total mechanical energy remains constant. In straight-and-level unaccelerated flight, both altitude and airspeed remain constant.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p93_6.png" alt="Constant energy state in straight-and-level flight" />

### 4. Energy Exchange ($h \leftrightarrow V$)
Altitude and airspeed can be exchanged without altering engine thrust:
* **Pitch Up (Trading Airspeed for Altitude):** Kinetic energy converts to potential energy. The airplane climbs while decelerating.
* **Pitch Down (Trading Altitude for Airspeed):** Potential energy converts to kinetic energy. The airplane descends while accelerating.

Absent any change in net energy ($T - D$), altitude and airspeed move in opposite directions during an energy exchange: as one increases, the other must decrease.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p94_7.png" alt="Exchanging potential and kinetic energy through pitch control" />

> **Attention:** Attempting to gain altitude without adequate thrust ($T < D$) rapidly depletes kinetic energy. If uncorrected, this can cause the aircraft to exceed the critical Angle of Attack and enter an aerodynamic **Stall**.

## Safety Considerations

Misinterpreting or neglecting the aircraft's energy state leads to common critical errors:

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p95_8.png" alt="Low energy state warning on final approach" />

* **Low Energy on Final Approach:** Allowing airspeed to decay below target while low on the glidepath leaves the aircraft with a combined deficit of potential and kinetic energy. Adding back elevator pressure without applying thrust will increase drag and worsen the sink rate.
* **High Density Altitude Operations:** High **Density Altitude** reduces engine power output (lower available thrust) and diminishes aerodynamic performance, reducing the specific excess power available for climb.
* **Stall/Spin Awareness:** Energy exchange has aerodynamic boundaries. You cannot indefinitely exchange airspeed for altitude without reaching the stalling Angle of Attack.

---

## Summary

* Energy management is the continuous planning, monitoring, and controlling of potential energy (altitude) and kinetic energy (airspeed).
* The aircraft's frame of reference is based on **Indicated Altitude** and **Indicated Airspeed (IAS)**.
* Net energy change is governed by the relationship between **Thrust** and **Drag** ($T - D$).
* When $T > D$, total energy increases (climb or acceleration). When $T < D$, total energy decreases (descent or deceleration).
* Trading airspeed for altitude without adjusting power preserves total mechanical energy temporarily, but drains kinetic energy and increases Angle of Attack toward a stall.
