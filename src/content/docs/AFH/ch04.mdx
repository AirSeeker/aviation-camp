## Introduction

Energy management is the process of planning, monitoring, and controlling altitude and airspeed targets in relation to the airplane's energy state. Mastering energy management enables a pilot to:

1. Attain and maintain desired vertical flightpath-airspeed profiles (such as a constant-airspeed climb or stabilized descent).
2. Detect, correct, and prevent unintentional altitude and airspeed deviations.
3. Prevent an irreversible loss of airspeed or an excessive sink rate that could result in an accident.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p88_1.png" alt="Overview of aircraft energy management concepts" />

> **Attention:** Mismanagement of mechanical energy (altitude and airspeed) is a leading contributing factor to aviation's three most common fatal accident categories: Loss of Control In-Flight (LOC-I), Controlled Flight Into Terrain (CFIT), and Approach-and-Landing Accidents.

---

## The Airplane as an Energy System

To manage an airplane effectively, a pilot must visualize the aircraft as an open energy system. Total mechanical energy consists of two primary components:

$$\text{Total Mechanical Energy} = \text{Potential Energy} + \text{Kinetic Energy}$$

$$\text{Total Energy} = mgh + \frac{1}{2}mV^2$$

Where:
* $m$ = mass (aircraft weight divided by gravity)
* $g$ = acceleration due to gravity
* $h$ = height (altitude)
* $V$ = velocity (airspeed)

Because an airplane is an **open energy system**, energy can be introduced into the system (from the fuel tanks via engine Thrust) and dissipated into the surrounding environment (through aerodynamic Drag).

### Frame of Reference for Managing Energy

A pilot’s frame of reference for managing the aircraft energy state is **airplane-centric**:
* **Potential energy** is referenced to **indicated altitude** (mean sea level or MSL), rather than height above ground level (AGL), which varies with terrain.
* **Kinetic energy** is referenced to **Indicated Airspeed (IAS)**, rather than Groundspeed, which varies with wind speed and direction.

While external factors like terrain elevation and wind dictate operational risks, the pilot directly controls mechanical energy through flight control and throttle inputs that govern airspeed and altitude.

> **Attention:** The pilot’s primary instrument references for energy state are the altimeter (indicated altitude) and the airspeed indicator (Indicated Airspeed), not height AGL or groundspeed.

## Energy Balance and Net Energy Change

At any moment in flight, energy flows continuously into and out of the aircraft:
* **Energy In:** Engine Thrust ($T$)
* **Energy Out:** Aerodynamic Drag ($D$)

The net rate of energy change is determined by the difference between thrust and drag ($T - D$). This relationship is often measured as **Specific Excess Power ($P_S$)**:

$$P_S = \frac{(T - D)V}{W}$$

Where $W$ is aircraft weight and $V$ is velocity.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch04/img_p89_2.png" alt="Energy balance equation and transactions" />

### Typical Energy Transactions

Depending on the relationship between thrust and drag, several energy states can occur:

| Condition | Net Energy Change ($T - D$) | Altitude Stored Energy | Airspeed Stored Energy | Resulting Flight Condition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Surplus Energy** | $T - D > 0$ | Increase | No change | Climb at constant airspeed |
| **Surplus Energy** | $T - D > 0$ | No change | Increase | Acceleration at constant altitude |
| **Energy Deficit** | $T - D < 0$ | Decrease | No change | Descent at constant airspeed |
| **Energy Deficit** | $T - D < 0$ | No change | Decrease | Deceleration at constant altitude |
| **Equilibrium** | $T - D = 0$ | No change | No change | Constant altitude and airspeed |
| **Energy Trade** | $T - D \approx 0$ | Increase | Decrease | Climb with deceleration (trading airspeed for altitude) |

### Energy Exchange (Trading Altitude and Airspeed)

A pilot can trade potential energy for kinetic energy, or vice versa, without changing the total mechanical energy:
* **Trading Airspeed for Altitude:** Pitching up trades kinetic energy (airspeed drops) for potential energy (altitude increases).
* **Trading Altitude for Airspeed:** Pitching down trades potential energy (altitude drops) for kinetic energy (airspeed increases).

> **Attention:** Exchanging altitude for airspeed (or vice versa) redistributes stored energy, but it does not add total energy to the aircraft. Total energy increases only when engine Thrust exceeds Drag ($T > D$).

---

## Practical Application

Effective energy management requires coordinating pitch attitude and throttle settings:

1. **Power Controls Total Energy State:** Throttle adjustments change the net energy flow ($T - D$). Adding power introduces energy into the system to climb or accelerate; reducing power removes energy to descend or slow down.
2. **Pitch Controls Energy Distribution:** The elevator controls Angle of Attack and allocates available energy between altitude (potential) and airspeed (kinetic).
3. **Coordinated Level-Offs and Profile Changes:** Anticipating the lag between control inputs and aircraft response prevents overshooting target altitudes and airspeeds.

## Safety Considerations: Low-Energy Hazards

Mismanaging aircraft energy can quickly lead to hazardous flight conditions:

* **Low-Energy State on Approach:** Operating at a low airspeed combined with a high sink rate requires substantial excess power to recover. If the engine is at idle or recovery is delayed, the aircraft may stall or impact the ground short of the runway.
* **Loss of Control In-Flight (LOC-I):** Trading airspeed for altitude without adequate engine thrust can cause the aircraft to exceed the critical Angle of Attack, triggering an uncoordinated Stall or spin.
* **Controlled Flight Into Terrain (CFIT):** Failing to establish a positive rate of climb when approaching rising terrain occurs when the aircraft lacks sufficient Specific Excess Power to out-climb the obstacle.

---

## Summary

* Energy management is the active planning, monitoring, and controlling of potential energy (altitude) and kinetic energy (airspeed).
* The aircraft is an open energy system where Thrust adds energy and Drag expends energy.
* Net energy change is governed by $T - D$. A surplus allows climbing or accelerating; a deficit results in descending or decelerating.
* Altitude and airspeed can be exchanged for one another, but total energy can only increase when Thrust exceeds Drag.
* Sound energy management is essential for preventing LOC-I, CFIT, and approach-and-landing accidents.
