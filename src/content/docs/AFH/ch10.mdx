## Introduction

Basic flight training instills the fundamentals of flight: straight-and-level, turns, climbs, and descents. As flight training advances, performance maneuvers are introduced to develop higher levels of pilot skill, flight control smoothness, maneuver planning, situational awareness, and division of attention. 

Performance maneuvers require the pilot to apply continuously changing flight control pressures across varying attitudes, airspeeds, and spatial orientations. Mastery of these maneuvers demands consistent competency in basic flight fundamentals, thorough ground preparation, and segmented, building-block instruction.

## Aerodynamic Principles of Steep Turns

A steep turn consists of single or multiple 360° or 720° turns performed in either or both directions, using a bank angle between 45° and 60°. While the fundamental principles governing turns remain unchanged, steep turns expose the pilot to unique aerodynamic and physiological factors:

1. **Increased Load Factor (G-forces):** In a level turn, the total Lift produced divides into a vertical component of Lift and a horizontal component of Lift. To maintain altitude, the pilot must increase the Angle of Attack (AOA) to maintain sufficient vertical Lift to support the aircraft's Weight. The vector addition of gravity and centrifugal force creates the load factor:
   - At a **45° bank**, the load factor is **1.41 G**.
   - At a **60° bank**, the load factor is **2.0 G** (the effective weight of the aircraft and occupants doubles).
   - Above 60° of bank, load factor climbs exponentially. A level 75° banked turn exceeds the 3.8 G structural limit of normal category general aviation airplanes.

2. **Increased Stalling Speed:** Stalling speed increases proportional to the square root of the load factor ($\sqrt{n}$). For example, an airplane with a level-flight stalling speed of 50 knots stalls at approximately 60 knots at 45° of bank, and at 70 knots at 60° of bank.

3. **Overbanking Tendency:** At steep angles of bank, the outside wing travels at a slightly higher airspeed through the air than the inside wing, generating greater Lift. This causes the airplane to continue rolling into an increasingly steep bank unless deliberate, slight opposite aileron pressure is maintained throughout the turn.

4. **Induced Drag and Power Requirements:** Raising the Angle of Attack to maintain vertical lift increases induced Drag. Additional engine power must be added upon entry to prevent a decay in Indicated Airspeed (IAS).

> **Attention:** Steep turns must be initiated at an airspeed that does not exceed the airplane's design maneuvering speed ($V_A$) or operating maneuvering speed ($V_O$). At or below this speed, the airplane will stall before exceeding structural design load limits.

## Practical Application: Executing the Steep Turn

### 1. Preparation and Clearing
Before beginning any performance maneuver, verify that the area is clear of other aircraft and terrain hazards by executing clearing turns. Choose a distinct outside visual reference point (such as a prominent landmark or mountain peak on the horizon) to align entry and time the rollout.

### 2. Entry and Roll-In
- Establish the manufacturer's recommended entry speed, or an airspeed at or below $V_A$/$V_O$.
- Note the entry Heading and Altitude.
- Smoothly roll into the predetermined bank angle (between 45° and 60°).
- As the bank passes through approximately 30°, smoothly apply elevator back pressure to increase Angle of Attack, and advance the throttle to maintain Indicated Airspeed (IAS).

> **Attention:** Be prepared for left-turning tendencies such as P-factor during high power, high Angle of Attack configurations. Maintain coordinated flight with coordinated rudder inputs.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch10/img_p214_3.png" alt="Cockpit instrument panel indications and outside visual horizon reference during a steep turn" />

### 3. Sustained Turn and Scanning
- **Visual References:** Do not focus solely on the nose. Monitor both the nose and wingtips relative to the natural horizon to sense Pitch and Bank trends instantly.
- **Instrument Cross-Check:** Rapidly scan between outside references and flight instruments (altimeter, attitude indicator, airspeed indicator, and turn coordinator).
- **Elevator Trim:** Trimming elevator pressure during a steep turn is optional and depends on pilot technique and aircraft flight characteristics. If elevator trim is added, it must be smoothly removed before or during the rollout.

### 4. Altitude Corrections
Altitude deviations are common during steep turns:
- **Minor Altitude Loss:** Apply slight back-elevator pressure while maintaining the bank angle.
- **Significant Altitude Loss:** Do **not** simply pull back on the yoke; doing so tightens the turn, increases load factor, and steepens the descent. First, reduce the bank angle slightly with coordinated aileron and rudder, pull up smoothly to re-establish level pitch attitude, and then roll back to the target bank angle.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch10/img_p215_4.png" alt="Rollout and pitch correction sequence for steep turns" />

### 5. Rollout
- Begin rolling out toward the entry heading approximately half the bank angle before the target (e.g., lead the heading by 20° to 25° for a 45° to 50° bank).
- Smoothly neutralize elevator back pressure and reduce power to cruise settings as the wings level.
- If trim was added during the turn, neutralize it simultaneously to avoid an abrupt climb on rollout.

## Common Errors to Avoid

> **Attention:** Fixating entirely inside the cockpit on the altimeter or attitude indicator leads to over-controlling, poor coordination, and a loss of visual collision-avoidance scanning.

- Failing to thoroughly clear the practice area prior to maneuver entry.
- Inadequate back-elevator pressure upon passing 30° of bank, leading to altitude loss.
- Excessive back-elevator pressure resulting in an uncoordinated climb or approaching an accelerated Stall.
- Neglecting to add engine power, allowing airspeed to decay.
- Over-controlling or failing to correct for overbanking tendency.
- Pitching up abruptly without reducing bank when recovering from an altitude deviation.
- Forgetting to relieve elevator back pressure and trim upon rollout, resulting in an immediate altitude balloon.

---

## Summary

Performance maneuvers refine pilot coordination, anticipation, and situational awareness. Executing steep turns requires understanding how load factor, stall speed, and induced drag escalate with bank angle. Maintaining coordinated controls, dividing attention between outside visual references and internal flight instruments, and promptly recognizing overbanking tendency ensure precise, safe flight operations.
