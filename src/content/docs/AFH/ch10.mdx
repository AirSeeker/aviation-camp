## Introduction

Basic flight maneuvers teach pilots how to control an airplane in straight-and-level flight, climbs, descents, and standard medium-banked turns. As flight training advances, performance maneuvers develop a pilot's proficiency to a much higher standard. These maneuvers refine flight control application, maneuver planning, situational awareness, and the continuous division of attention between flight instruments and outside references.

Performance maneuvers introduce dynamic flight environments where control pressures, attitudes, airspeeds, and spatial orientations change continuously. Mastery of these maneuvers requires a solid foundation in the fundamental flight skills. Initial training begins with ground preparation to understand the aerodynamic principles before executing them in the cockpit through structured, progressive practice.

## Core Aerodynamic Concepts of Steep Turns

A **Steep Turn** consists of a $360^\circ$ or $720^\circ$ turn in either direction (or successive turns in opposite directions) using a bank angle between $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$. While the fundamental physics of a turn remain consistent regardless of bank, steep turns introduce unique aerodynamic phenomena that require deliberate control inputs.

### Division of Lift and Load Factor

When an airplane enters a banked turn, the total lift vector tilts:

* **Vertical Component of Lift:** Opposes weight and keeps the airplane level.
* **Horizontal Component of Lift:** Acts toward the center of the turn (centripetal force) to turn the aircraft.

As bank angle increases, the vertical component of lift decreases significantly. To maintain a constant altitude, the pilot must increase the **Angle of Attack** (AOA) by applying back-elevator pressure. 

<img src="/images/AFH/ch10/img_p212_2.png" alt="Vector resolution of lift in steep turns and resulting load factor" />

Because the pilot increases AOA to maintain altitude against both gravity and centrifugal force, the wings support an increased effective load factor ($G$):

$$\text{Load Factor } (G) = \frac{1}{\cos(\text{Bank Angle})}$$

* At a **$45^\circ$ bank**, the load factor is **$1.41\text{ G}$**.
* At a **$60^\circ$ bank**, the load factor reaches **$2.0\text{ G}$**, effectively doubling the weight of the aircraft and its occupants.
* Beyond $60^\circ$, load factors climb exponentially. A level turn at $75^\circ$ bank requires roughly $3.86\text{ G}$, exceeding the maximum design limit load of standard category aircraft ($+3.8\text{ G}$).

> **Attention:** Because load factor increases with bank angle, stalling speed also increases proportional to the square root of the load factor:
> 
> $$V_{\text{stall, accelerated}} = V_{\text{stall, level}} \times \sqrt{\text{Load Factor}}$$
> 
> An airplane that stalls at $50\text{ knots}$ in level flight will stall at approximately $60\text{ knots}$ in a $45^\circ$ level turn and at approximately $70\text{ knots}$ in a $60^\circ$ turn.

### Maneuvering Speed Limitations

Steep turns must be entered at or below the design maneuvering speed ($V_A$) or operating maneuvering speed ($V_O$). At or below this speed, the airplane will stall aerodynamically before the wings exceed their design limit load factor, protecting the airframe from structural overstress.

### Overbanking Tendency and Adverse Yaw

In shallow turns, aircraft exhibit positive spiral stability. However, at steep bank angles ($45^\circ$ to $60^\circ$), the outside wing travels faster through the air than the inside wing because it describes a larger arc. The outside wing generates more lift, causing an **overbanking tendency**. The airplane will continue to increase its bank angle unless the pilot applies deliberate, opposite aileron pressure to hold the bank steady.

Simultaneously, the pilot must counteract left-turning tendencies (such as P-factor and gyroscopic precession) with coordinated rudder pressure to maintain coordinated flight throughout the maneuver.

## Practical Application: Executing the Steep Turn

<img src="/images/AFH/ch10/img_p214_3.png" alt="Visual horizon references during a steep turn" />

### 1. Preparation and Clearing Turns
* Perform clearing turns to ensure the practice area is free of traffic and obstructions.
* Select prominent visual ground and horizon references to identify the rollout heading.
* Configure the airplane at the recommended entry airspeed, ensuring it is at or below $V_A$ or $V_O$.

### 2. Entry and Roll-In
* Smoothly roll into the predetermined bank angle ($45^\circ$ for Private Pilot standards; up to $50^\circ$ or $60^\circ$ for Commercial Pilot standards).
* As the bank passes through approximately $30^\circ$, smoothly add elevator back pressure to increase AOA and maintain level altitude.
* Advance power slightly to overcome the induced drag resulting from the increased AOA and maintain a constant **Indicated Airspeed (IAS)**.

> **Attention:** If elevator trim is used to relieve heavy control yoke pressures, pilots must actively remove the trim and reduce power during rollout. Failing to relieve back-trim before rolling wings level causes an abrupt, unintended climb.

### 3. Maintaining Attitude and Scanning
* Establish an outside visual scan by observing both the nose and the wingtips relative to the horizon. Looking only at the nose makes pitch and altitude deviations difficult to detect.
* Maintain cross-check division of attention: 80–90% outside scanning for pitch attitude, bank reference, and traffic; 10–20% inside scanning the altimeter, attitude indicator, and airspeed indicator.
* Apply subtle opposite aileron pressure to stop the overbanking tendency.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch10/img_p215_4.png" alt="Correcting altitude and bank errors during steep turns" />

### 4. Correcting Altitude Deviations
* **If altitude is lost:** Do not pull straight back on the elevator while banked steeply. Pulling back in a steep bank tightens the turn radius and increases the rate of descent. Instead, slightly reduce the bank angle first, smoothly pull the nose up to level the pitch attitude, and then return to the target bank angle.
* **If altitude is gained:** Increase bank angle slightly or reduce back-elevator pressure slightly, then re-establish the target parameters.

### 5. Rollout and Recovery
* Lead the rollout heading by approximately one-half the bank angle (e.g., lead by $20^\circ$ to $25^\circ$ for a $45^\circ$ to $50^\circ$ bank).
* Simultaneously release back-elevator pressure, roll wings level using coordinated rudder and aileron, and reduce power back to cruise settings.
* If performing consecutive steep turns in opposite directions, smoothly reverse control inputs to roll into the opposite bank without pausing in level flight.

## Common Pilot Errors

* Inadequate clearing turns or poor visual scan before and during the maneuver.
* Failure to increase pitch attitude and engine power as the bank angle exceeds $30^\circ$.
* Staring inside at the instrument panel rather than maintaining primary visual reference to the natural horizon.
* Overcontrolling the elevator or attempting to regain lost altitude without first shallowing the bank.
* Allowing overbanking tendency to steepen the turn uncontrollably.
* Rolling out early or late relative to the selected visual reference or heading.

---

## Safety Considerations

* **Structural Integrity:** Never perform steep turns above $V_A$ or $V_O$. Excessively abrupt pitch inputs or gusts at high bank angles can overstress the airframe.
* **Accelerated Stalls:** High load factors raise the stalling speed significantly. Maintain sufficient airspeed and avoid abrupt aft cyclic/elevator movements that could exceed the critical angle of attack.
* **Disorientation:** Rapid transitions between high load factors and varied roll attitudes can cause spatial disorientation. Keep eyes moving across the outside horizon and cross-check the flight instruments.

---

## Summary

Performance maneuvers like steep turns build precise aircraft handling, visual orientation, and flight control coordination. By anticipating the loss of vertical lift, compensating for overbanking tendency, managing high load factors, and dividing attention between inside instruments and outside references, pilots achieve the finesse and responsiveness required for advanced flight operations.
