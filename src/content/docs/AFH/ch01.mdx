## Introduction

The primary objective of flight training is the acquisition and honing of basic **airmanship skills**. Airmanship represents a broad synthesis of flight discipline: a sound foundation in aerodynamic principles, the ability to operate an aircraft with precision and competence both on the ground and in the air, and the consistent exercise of sound judgment to maximize operational safety.

Although learning to fly is frequently compared to learning to drive an automobile, this analogy is fundamentally misleading. Automobiles operate in a two-dimensional plane, whereas aircraft maneuver through a dynamic three-dimensional environment. Consequently, operating an airplane requires acute sensory awareness, nuanced psychomotor control, and constant situational vigilance.

## Core Concepts: Foundations of Airmanship

Mastering the control of an aircraft demands the development of four distinct, interrelated motor and sensory capabilities:

* **Coordination:** The ability to manipulate hand controls (yoke or stick) and foot controls (rudder pedals) simultaneously and subconsciously in the correct relationship to achieve coordinated flight without adverse yaw or slippage.
* **Timing:** The application of control inputs at the precise instant required to produce smooth, continuous, and predictable aircraft maneuvers.
* **Control Touch:** The intuitive ability to assess aircraft attitude, speed variations, and dynamic air loads by sensing the varying pressures and resistance transmitted through the flight controls to the pilot's hands and feet.
* **Speed Sense:** The conscious and subconscious ability to react to changes in Indicated Airspeed (IAS) by assessing aerodynamic sound, control feel, and visual attitude cues.

### Attributes of an Accomplished Pilot

Safe, proficient aviators do not merely execute physical stick-and-rudder tasks; they exercise disciplined cognitive and risk management skills. An accomplished pilot demonstrates the ability to:

1. Assess complex, unfolding situations quickly and select the proper procedure.
2. Predict the probable consequences of a flight action or environmental condition.
3. Maintain vigilant regard for operational safety at all times.
4. Accurately gauge aircraft performance capabilities and environmental limits.
5. Recognize personal minimums and aircraft limitations, deliberately avoiding situations that exceed them.
6. Identify, evaluate, and mitigate flight hazards on a continuous basis.

> **Attention:** The ultimate goal of flight training is to develop a safe, competent, and self-reliant pilot. Successfully passing the practical test (checkride) for a pilot certificate is merely an incidental milestone along that path.

### Transferability of Flight Skills

Every make and model of aircraft possesses unique control responses and operational quirks. However, the objective of primary and intermediate training is not simply learning to pilot one specific model. Instead, training instills universal core competencies, habits, and foundational skills that transfer seamlessly to higher-performance, multi-engine, or complex airplanes.

## Regulatory Framework: Role of the FAA

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is empowered by the United States Congress to foster civil aviation safety by establishing standards for airman certification, aircraft manufacturing, airworthiness, and airspace operations. 

These standards are codified in **Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR)**, historically referred to as the Federal Aviation Regulations (FARs). 

<img src="/images/AFH/ch01/img_p24_3.png" alt="Title 14 CFR Chapter 1 organization and subchapters" />

### Key Parts of 14 CFR for Flight Training

* **Part 1:** Definitions and Abbreviations.
* **Part 61:** Certification of Pilots, Flight Instructors, and Ground Instructors. Prescribes the minimum aeronautical knowledge, flight proficiency, and experience requirements to obtain pilot certificates and ratings.
* **Part 67 & Part 68:** Medical Standards and Certification, and the alternative operational framework (BasicMed).
* **Part 91:** General Operating and Flight Rules. Governs all civil aviation operating rules within U.S. airspace, including equipment requirements, flight rules (VFR/IFR), and general maintenance requirements.
* **Part 141 & Part 142:** Pilot Schools and Training Centers offering structured, FAA-approved curricula.

## Aircraft Certification and Airworthiness

Airworthiness is governed across several interdependent parts of 14 CFR:

* **14 CFR Part 21:** Prescribes procedural requirements for issuing type certificates, production approvals, and airworthiness certificates. 
* **14 CFR Part 39 (Airworthiness Directives):** Grants the FAA legal authority to issue mandatory Airworthiness Directives (ADs) when an unsafe condition is identified in an aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance that is likely to exist or develop in other products of identical design.
* **14 CFR Part 43:** Sets the legal rules for maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alterations performed on U.S.-certificated aircraft.
* **14 CFR Part 45:** Governs aircraft identification, engine and component data plates, and nationality and registration marking (tail numbers).

<img src="/images/AFH/ch01/img_p25_4.png" alt="FAA Standard Airworthiness Certificate Form 8100-2" />

> **Attention:** Under 14 CFR Part 91, Section 91.203(b), a valid **Standard Airworthiness Certificate (FAA Form 8100-2)** must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance such that it is legible to passengers or crew.

> **Attention:** Compliance with FAA Airworthiness Directives (ADs) is mandatory. Unlike manufacturer service bulletins, non-compliance with an applicable AD renders the aircraft unairworthy and legally grounded under 14 CFR Part 39.

### Maintaining Airworthiness Validity

A Standard Airworthiness Certificate remains valid only as long as:
1. The aircraft conforms to its approved type design.
2. The aircraft is in a safe condition for operation.
3. All required maintenance, preventive maintenance, inspections, and alterations are performed and documented in accordance with 14 CFR Parts 21, 43, and 91.

## Practical Application & Habit Formation

Safe aviation practices originate on the first training flight:

* **Developing "Control Touch" Early:** Avoid over-controlling or rigidly gripping the flight controls. A relaxed, light touch allows you to perceive subtle changes in aerodynamic forces, control effectiveness, and trim changes as airspeed changes.
* **Active Horizon Scanning:** Cross-reference flight instruments with the outside visual horizon. This solidifies the habit of flying by outside visual references while maintaining situational awareness and collision avoidance scans.
* **Preflight Regulatory Verification:** Before every flight, confirm that the aircraft has all required documentation on board (Airworthiness Certificate, Registration, Operating Limitations/POH, and Weight and Balance) and verify that all required periodic inspections and ADs are complied with.

---

## Summary

Flight training builds fundamental airmanship skills—coordination, timing, control touch, and speed sense—within a three-dimensional operational environment. Accomplished pilots combine these psychomotor skills with ongoing risk management, aeronautical decision-making, and disciplined adherence to 14 CFR regulations. Mastering these core principles creates safe operational habits transferable to any category or class of aircraft.
