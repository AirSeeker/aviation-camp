## Introduction

Experienced pilots place a strong emphasis on ground operations because safe flight begins and ends on the ground. Hasty ground operations diminish safety margins. Ground preparation provides the pilot with the dedicated time needed to evaluate regulatory requirements, personal fitness for flight, aircraft mechanical condition, environmental factors, and potential external pressures.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch02/img_p39_2.png" alt="Pilot conducting a visual preflight inspection of an aircraft" />

A pilot's situational awareness begins during initial preflight preparation and does not conclude until the aircraft is secured at its tie-down or hangar, or until a sound decision is made to cancel the flight.

---

## Aircraft Airworthiness and Pilot Responsibilities

Before every flight, the pilot must ensure the aircraft meets regulatory standards and is in a condition for safe operation.

Under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 3, section 3.5(a), **airworthy** means that the aircraft conforms to its type design and is in a condition for safe operation.

Two distinct responsibilities govern airworthiness:
- **Owner/Operator Responsibility:** Under 14 CFR part 91, the owner or operator is primarily responsible for maintaining the aircraft in an airworthy condition.
- **Pilot-in-Command (PIC) Responsibility:** Under 14 CFR part 91, section 91.7, no person may operate a civil aircraft unless it is in an airworthy condition. The pilot-in-command is directly responsible for determining whether the aircraft is safe for flight. If an unsafe condition occurs during flight, the PIC must discontinue the flight.

> **Attention:** Even if maintenance records indicate that all inspections are current, the pilot-in-command bears final responsibility for verifying that the aircraft is mechanically sound and safe to fly before takeoff.

## Required Maintenance Inspections and Aircraft Logbooks

Every aircraft has a set of logbooks covering the airframe, engine, and, where applicable, propeller and appliances. These logbooks document all maintenance, alterations, preventive maintenance, and mandatory inspections. 

Logbooks are typically kept in a secure location on the ground rather than inside the aircraft. Before flying, the pilot must review the maintenance records or an approved airworthiness status summary to confirm the following required inspections and checks are current:

<img src="/images/AFH/ch02/img_p38_1.png" alt="Reviewing aircraft maintenance logbooks" />

- **Annual Inspection:** Within the preceding 12 calendar months (14 CFR § 91.409(a)).
- **100-Hour Inspection:** Required if the aircraft is operated for hire or flight instruction for hire (14 CFR § 91.409(b)).
- **Transponder Certification:** Within the preceding 24 calendar months (14 CFR § 91.413).
- **Static System and Altimeter/Encoder Certification:** Within the preceding 24 calendar months for operations under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR) in controlled airspace (14 CFR § 91.411).
- **VOR Equipment Check:** Within the preceding 30 days for IFR flight using VOR navigation (14 CFR § 91.171).
- **Emergency Locator Transmitter (ELT) Inspection:** Within the preceding 12 calendar months (14 CFR § 91.207(d)).
- **ELT Battery Expiration:** Replacement required after 1 cumulative hour of use or when 50 percent of useful battery life has expired (14 CFR § 91.207(c)).
- **Airworthiness Directives (ADs):** Verification that all applicable ADs have been complied with and properly documented (14 CFR § 91.417).
- **Life-Limited Parts:** Verification of operating status against Type Certificate Data Sheets (TCDS).
- **Major Repairs and Alterations:** Proper recording on FAA Form 337.

## Required Aircraft Documents

The pilot must confirm that all required certificates, documents, and placards are on board the aircraft. These include:

1. **Airworthiness Certificate:** Must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so it is legible to passengers or crew (14 CFR § 91.203).
2. **Registration Certificate:** Current state or federal registration confirming ownership (14 CFR § 91.203).
3. **Radio Station License:** Required by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) for flights outside the United States or for aircraft weighing more than 12,500 pounds.
4. **Operating Limitations:** Contained in the FAA-approved Airplane Flight Manual (AFM) or Pilot's Operating Handbook (POH), along with required placards and instrument markings (14 CFR § 91.9).
5. **Weight and Balance Data:** Current and specific to the individual aircraft.
6. **Compass Deviation Card:** Mounted on or near the magnetic compass.
7. **External Data Plate:** Securely attached to the exterior fuselage (14 CFR § 45.11).

## Conducting the Preflight Visual Inspection

The preflight visual assessment mitigates hazards prior to engine start. The inspection should follow the manufacturer's checklist found in the AFM/POH.

### Ramp Walk-Up Assessment
The visual preflight inspection begins as the pilot approaches the aircraft on the ramp:
- Observe the general stance and appearance of the airplane.
- Check for signs of landing gear misalignment or structural sagging.
- Check for distortions, ripples, or wrinkles in the wings, fuselage, and empennage skin.
- Inspect the ground beneath the engine, fuel drains, and hydraulic lines for fresh stains, dripping fluids, or fuel and oil puddles.

### Cabin Entry and Structural Checks
The detailed inspection typically begins at the cabin door:
- **Door Operation:** Check that the door opens and closes smoothly and latches securely. A door that binds or does not align properly may indicate internal structural distortion or airframe damage caused by hard landings or overstress.
- **Control Locks and Switches:** Remove control locks, verify the master switch and ignition switches are in the proper position, and check fuel quantity indicators against the visual fuel levels.

> **Attention:** If a cabin door or emergency exit binds, latches with excessive resistance, or shows gap unevenness along the frame, inspect the surrounding doorpost and fuselage structure for hidden structural damage before flight.

---

## Safety Considerations and Risk Management

Rushing a preflight inspection is a common factor in preventable aviation accidents. Ground operations demand disciplined adherence to checklists:

- **Checklist Discipline:** Never perform a walkaround solely by memory. Use the manufacturer's checklist to maintain an orderly flow and ensure no critical items are missed.
- **External Pressures:** Resist pressures related to passenger schedules, deteriorating weather, or operational delays that encourage abbreviating preflight checks.
- **Inoperative Equipment:** If any equipment or indicator is found broken or missing during the preflight inspection, refer to 14 CFR § 91.213 and the aircraft equipment list before determining whether the flight can safely and legally proceed.

---

## Summary

- Safe flight begins and ends with thorough ground operations and methodical flight preparation.
- An aircraft is **airworthy** only when it conforms to its type design and is in condition for safe operation.
- The owner/operator is responsible for maintaining the aircraft, but the **pilot-in-command** is solely responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in safe condition for flight.
- Aircraft logbooks confirm compliance with required inspections, including the annual, 100-hour (if operated for hire), transponder, altimeter/static system, and ELT checks.
- Essential documents—including the Airworthiness Certificate, Registration Certificate, Operating Limitations (AFM/POH), and current Weight and Balance data—must be confirmed on board.
- The visual walkaround begins on the ramp approach and methodically checks the aircraft structure, systems, and controls using the AFM/POH checklist.
