## Introduction

Airport traffic patterns ensure that air traffic moves into and out of an airport smoothly and safely. Unlike automobiles on a roadway, airplanes cannot come to a complete stop in midair to yield right-of-way. Consequently, standard traffic patterns and standardized operating procedures are established to minimize conflicts during takeoffs, departures, arrivals, and landings.

The direction, layout, and altitude of an airport traffic pattern depend on the active runway, local wind conditions, terrain, obstructions, and noise abatement procedures. Comprehensive information for a specific airport is published in the FAA Chart Supplements and outlined in the *Aeronautical Information Manual* (AIM). Regardless of the type of airport—ranging from remote grass strips to complex towered terminals—adherence to standardized pattern procedures and continuous visual scanning under Visual Flight Rules (VFR) are fundamental to flight safety.

## Core Concepts of the Traffic Pattern

### Standard Pattern Geometry and Direction
The standard airport traffic pattern is a rectangular course with five distinct legs:
1. **Upwind Leg:** A flight path parallel to the landing runway in the direction of landing.
2. **Crosswind Leg:** A transitional horizontal flight path perpendicular to the landing runway.
3. **Downwind Leg:** A course flown parallel to the landing runway in the opposite direction of landing, approximately 1/2 to 1 mile out.
4. **Base Leg:** A transitional path perpendicular to the landing runway, connecting the downwind leg to the final approach course.
5. **Final Approach:** A descending flight path aligned with the runway centerline extending from the base leg to the runway threshold.

Unless visual markings or published airport procedures specify right-hand turns, **all turns in the traffic pattern must be made to the left**.

> **Attention:** Unless an airport displays approved visual indicators or regulatory notices requiring right-hand turns, always make traffic pattern turns to the left.

### Pattern Altitude and Airspeed
* **Traffic Pattern Altitude (TPA):** Established locally, TPA is traditionally flown at **1,000 feet above ground level (AGL)** above the airport elevation, unless otherwise published in the Chart Supplement. Flying a consistent altitude minimizes the risk of midair collisions, particularly at non-towered fields.
* **Airspeed Limitations:** In accordance with 14 CFR Part 91, aircraft operating in the traffic pattern of a primary airport in Class C or Class D airspace typically observe an airspeed limit of no greater than 200 knots Indicated Airspeed (IAS). At non-towered airports, pilots should operate at compatible speeds that accommodate lighter, slower traffic while maintaining safe margins above the aircraft's stall speed.

### Visual Aids and the Segmented Circle
At non-towered airports without active radio advisories, pilots must determine the active runway and pattern direction visually:
* **Wind Cone / Windsock:** Indicates wind direction and provides an estimate of wind velocity and gustiness. Airplanes take off and land into the wind (preferring a Headwind over a Tailwind).
* **Segmented Circle:** A circular ground marking placed near runways to draw attention to wind indicators and landing direction aids.
* **L-Shaped Indicators:** Installed on the segmented circle to indicate the traffic pattern direction. The long member of the "L" represents the runway, and the short member indicates the direction of the turn onto the base leg and final approach.

## Practical Application: Flying the Pattern

### 1. Pattern Entry
Before entering the pattern at a non-towered airport, pilots must verify pattern direction and TPA, and visually scan for traffic already established in the flow.

* **Standard Entry:** Fly to enter at a **45° angle to the downwind leg**, aiming toward a point abeam the midpoint of the landing runway.
* **Altitude Management:** Arrive at the traffic pattern altitude prior to entering the pattern entry leg. Never descend directly into a traffic pattern.

> **Attention:** Entering a traffic pattern while descending creates an extreme collision hazard by blinding the descending pilot to traffic flying beneath them. Level off at TPA well before joining the 45° entry leg.

### 2. Downwind Leg
* Maintain pattern altitude and establish a ground track 1/2 to 1 mile away from the runway, compensating for any Crosswind with appropriate crab angles.
* Complete the before-landing checklist and extend the landing gear if flying a retractable-gear airplane.
* Maintain pattern altitude until reaching a point **abeam the approach end** (threshold) of the landing runway.
* At the abeam point, reduce power, configure initial flaps if appropriate, and establish a stabilized descent.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch08/img_p169_3.png" alt="Pattern Entry Angles and Position Abeam the Runway Threshold" />

### 3. Base Leg
* When the runway threshold is approximately 45° behind the airplane's wing, initiate a medium-bank turn onto the base leg.
* Correct for Crosswind on base to ensure a perpendicular ground track. A tailwind on downwind becomes a crosswind pushing the aircraft toward the runway on base, requiring prompt heading adjustments.
* Continue the descent, adjust pitch to maintain the desired Indicated Airspeed (IAS), and configure additional flaps as required.

### 4. Final Approach and Departure
* Turn onto final approach to align directly with the extended runway centerline. Complete the turn at an altitude that permits a stabilized descent to the intended touchdown zone.
* On departure, maintain runway alignment on the upwind leg. If departing the pattern, continue straight out or exit with a 45° turn in the direction of the traffic pattern after reaching pattern altitude and passing beyond the departure end of the runway.

## Safety Considerations

* **See and Avoid:** Operating in a traffic pattern—even at a towered airport—does not relieve the pilot-in-command of the responsibility to see and avoid other aircraft from ramp-out to ramp-in.
* **Blind Spots During Turns:** High-wing airplanes lose visibility in the direction of the turn when banked; low-wing airplanes lose visibility below and opposite the banked wing. Clear the area visually before initiating turns onto base and final.
* **Stall/Spin Awareness:** Skidding turns from base to final—often caused by overshooting the runway centerline and applying excessive rudder—drastically increase Angle of Attack on the outer wing and can trigger a deadly low-altitude stall and spin. Always maintain coordinated flight.

> **Attention:** Never attempt to salvage an overshot base-to-final turn with excessive inside rudder. Keep the turn coordinated, maintain proper airspeed, or execute an immediate go-around.

---

## Summary

* Standard airport traffic patterns use left turns and an altitude of 1,000 feet AGL unless otherwise specified in Chart Supplements or indicated on ground visual aids.
* The standard entry is executed at a 45° angle to the downwind leg, pointing toward the midpoint of the landing runway, fully leveled at traffic pattern altitude.
* Segmented circles and L-shaped markers visually communicate runway orientation and traffic pattern turn directions.
* Pilots must remain vigilant with continuous outside scanning and maintain coordinated flight to prevent low-altitude stalls.
