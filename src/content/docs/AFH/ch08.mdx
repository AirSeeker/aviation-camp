## Introduction

Airport traffic patterns provide a standardized, orderly flow of air traffic entering, operating within, and departing an airport. Because aircraft cannot simply stop to yield right-of-way in midair, standard procedures and flight paths are critical to minimize traffic conflicts and reduce the risk of midair collisions.

Information regarding airport-specific traffic pattern altitudes, right-hand traffic patterns, or non-standard arrival procedures is published in the Chart Supplements and the Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM). Regardless of whether an airport is towered or non-towered, every pilot must adhere to traffic rules, maintain situational awareness, and continuously exercise the responsibility to "see and avoid" other aircraft from ramp-out to ramp-in.

---

## Core Concepts

### Standard Rectangular Pattern

The standard traffic pattern is rectangular, providing defined segments where pilots can configure their aircraft, manage Indicated Airspeed (IAS), visually scan for conflicting traffic, and establish a stabilized descent toward the landing runway.

Unless visual indicators, signs, or air traffic control instructions designate otherwise:
- **Direction of turns:** All turns in a standard traffic pattern are made to the **left**.
- **Traffic Pattern Altitude (TPA):** The pattern altitude is typically flown at **1,000 feet above ground level (AGL)** above the airport surface elevation.
- **Airspeed adjustments:** In accordance with 14 CFR Part 91, aircraft operating in the traffic pattern at most airports fly at airspeeds at or below 200 knots (230 mph). Pilots should adjust their airspeed appropriately to remain compatible with other aircraft in the pattern.

> **Attention:** At airports without an operating control tower, adhering strictly to the published Traffic Pattern Altitude and standard entry procedures is the most effective defense against midair collisions.

### Non-Towered vs. Towered Operations

- **Towered Airports:** Pilots receive specific clearances to enter the pattern, report on specific legs, or fly straight-in approaches. Air traffic controllers sequence traffic; however, pilots remain ultimately responsible for separation and collision avoidance.
- **Non-Towered Airports:** Inbound pilots must actively monitor Common Traffic Advisory Frequencies (CTAF), determine the active runway, identify existing traffic, and conform to the traffic pattern in use. If no aircraft are observed, pilots must determine the active runway from visual ground indicators before entering.

## Visual Ground Indicators

When arriving at an airport without an operating control tower and with no reported traffic, pilots must observe airport wind and traffic direction indicators from an altitude well above the traffic pattern before descending to pattern altitude.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch08/img_p167_2.png" alt="Segmented Circle and Traffic Pattern Indicators" />

### Segmented Circle and Traffic Indicators

Many non-towered airports employ a **segmented circle** layout adjacent to the runway:
- **Wind Direction Indicator:** A windsock, wind cone, or wind tee located in the center indicates the surface wind direction and velocity.
- **L-Shaped Traffic Pattern Indicators:** Arranged in pairs around the segmented circle. The long member of the "L" aligns parallel to the landing runway, while the short member indicates the direction of turns onto the base leg and final approach.

> **Attention:** Always overfly non-towered airports at least 500 to 1,000 feet above the traffic pattern altitude while inspecting the segmented circle and wind indicators to avoid descending into the traffic flow.

## Practical Application: Flying the Pattern

<img src="/images/AFH/ch08/img_p169_3.png" alt="Traffic Pattern Entry and Downwind Configuration" />

### Pattern Entry

The preferred standard entry into a rectangular traffic pattern is at a **45° angle to the downwind leg**, aimed at a point abeam the midpoint of the landing runway:
1. Arrive at the entry point already established at the designated Traffic Pattern Altitude.
2. Ensure the 45° entry leg is long enough to provide an unobstructed view of the entire pattern.
3. Merge smoothly into the downwind flow while scanning for conflicting aircraft.

### Downwind Leg

The downwind leg is flown parallel to the landing runway in the direction opposite to the landing direction:
- Maintain a lateral spacing of approximately **1/2 to 1 mile** from the runway.
- Fly at the designated traffic pattern altitude until at least abeam the approach end (threshold) of the runway.
- Complete the before-landing checklist and extend retractable landing gear (if applicable).
- Abeam the landing threshold, reduce engine power, configure initial flaps if appropriate, and establish a stabilized descent.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch08/img_p170_4.png" alt="Base Leg and Final Approach Transition" />

### Base Leg and Final Approach

- **Base Leg:** Turn onto the base leg when the runway threshold is approximately 45° behind the airplane's wing. The base leg serves as the primary transitional segment to assess descent rate, adjust flap settings, and compensate for any Crosswind drift to prevent overshooting the runway centerline.
- **Final Approach:** Turn from base onto the extended runway centerline at an altitude sufficient to complete a stabilized glide to the intended touchdown zone. Align the longitudinal axis with the runway and maintain target approach airspeed.

## Safety Considerations

```
                        [ Midpoint ]
Downwind Entry (45°) --------> ============================== (Runway)
                               [ Downwind Leg - 1,000' AGL ]
                                             |
                                             v  (Abeam Threshold: Reduce Power)
                                    [ Base Leg Turn (45°) ]
                                             |
                                             v
                                  [ Final Approach Leg ]
```

- **Descending Pattern Entries:** Entering the traffic pattern while descending creates an extreme collision hazard. Faster descending aircraft have poor downward visibility and can easily descend onto an aircraft already established on downwind. Level off at pattern altitude before joining the 45° entry.
- **Airspeed and Spacing:** Maintain a safe following distance from preceding aircraft. Avoid steep bank angles on the turn from base to final, which increase load factor and raise the Stall speed.
- **Continuous Visual Scan:** Even when self-announcing positions on CTAF, never assume all aircraft are radio-equipped or on frequency. Maintain continuous visual scanning throughout all pattern segments.

> **Attention:** Over-banking or skidding uncoordinated turns during the base-to-final turn significantly raises the risk of a cross-control Stall and spin at an altitude insufficient for recovery.

---

## Summary

- The standard traffic pattern is rectangular with left-hand turns and a pattern altitude typically 1,000 feet AGL.
- Standard pattern entry is flown at a 45° angle to the downwind leg, intercepting abeam the midpoint of the runway at pattern altitude.
- Descent begins on the downwind leg abeam the threshold, with the turn to base initiated approximately 45° from the runway threshold.
- Segmented circle indicators, windsocks, and the Chart Supplements provide essential information regarding runway alignment and non-standard right-hand patterns.
