## Introduction

Approximately twenty percent of all general aviation (GA) accidents occur during the takeoff and departure climb phases of flight. More than half of these accidents stem from pilot failure, with a significant number involving loss of control. Although the takeoff and initial climb represent a comparatively brief portion of an entire flight profile, pilot workload is exceptionally intense. 

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p134_1.png" alt="Takeoff and departure climb profile showing ground roll, lift-off, and initial climb phases" />

Takeoffs often present the greatest number of operational hazards encountered during normal operations. A thorough understanding of aerodynamics, aircraft systems, and operational procedures—combined with stick-and-rudder proficiency—is vital to flight safety.

> **Attention:** The procedures discussed in this chapter apply primarily to airplanes equipped with tricycle landing gear. Always consult the FAA-approved Airplane Flight Manual or Pilot’s Operating Handbook (AFM/POH) for specific airspeeds, configurations, and procedures. If information in this handbook conflicts with the AFM/POH, the manufacturer’s guidance takes precedence.

---

## Core Concepts and Definitions

Although the takeoff and climb is executed as one smooth, continuous maneuver, it is divided into three distinct operational phases for training and analysis:

1. **Takeoff Roll (Ground Roll):** The portion of the maneuver where the airplane accelerates from a complete stop to an Indicated Airspeed (IAS) capable of generating sufficient Lift for flight.
2. **Lift-off (Rotation):** The transition point where the wings generate enough Lift to support the Weight of the airplane. In nose-wheel equipped aircraft, the pilot rotates the nose upward to increase the Angle of Attack (AOA).
3. **Initial Climb:** Begins the moment the wheels leave the runway surface and a stabilized climb Pitch attitude is established. This phase is considered complete once the airplane reaches a safe maneuvering altitude or transitions into an en route climb profile.

## Pre-Takeoff Planning and Preparation

Safe departure operations begin well before entering the cockpit. Detailed performance calculations, runway assessments, and departure briefings mitigate critical risks before the throttle is advanced.

### Performance and Density Altitude Considerations

Before departure, pilots must consult the AFM/POH performance charts to verify that the available runway length exceeds the required takeoff ground roll and obstacle clearance distances.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p134_2.png" alt="Takeoff distance and rate of climb performance charts illustrating the impact of density altitude" />

Environmental conditions profoundly affect takeoff capability:
* **High Density Altitude:** High elevations, elevated ambient temperatures, and low atmospheric pressures reduce air density. This degrades engine power output, decreases propeller efficiency, and requires a higher True Airspeed (TAS) to produce the necessary Lift, resulting in significantly longer ground rolls and reduced climb rates.
* **Wind Components:** Takeoffs should be planned into the wind whenever possible. A Headwind provides instantaneous Indicated Airspeed prior to brake release, reducing the Groundspeed required to achieve flying speed. Consequently, a headwind shortens the ground roll, minimizes wear on the landing gear tires, and improves the climb angle relative to ground obstacles.

> **Attention:** Operating with a Tailwind dramatically lengthens the required takeoff distance and decreases the initial climb gradient over ground obstacles. Takeoffs with a tailwind must only be conducted if permitted by the AFM/POH and supported by adequate runway length and obstacle clearance margins.

### Pre-Takeoff Checklist and Briefing

Prior to taxiing onto the active runway:
* Complete all engine run-up checks and pre-takeoff checklists.
* Verify all engine instruments read within normal operating ranges.
* Verify that flight controls move freely, smoothly, and in the correct direction.
* Scan the final approach and departure corridors to confirm they are clear of conflicting traffic.
* At non-towered fields, broadcast intentions clearly on the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF); at towered airports, verify an explicit takeoff clearance has been received.

### Departure Emergency Briefing

Every departure requires a deliberate briefing of contingency actions in the event of an abnormal situation:
* **Failure Prior to Rotation:** Abort the takeoff immediately by closing the throttle and applying maximum braking to stop on the remaining runway.
* **Engine Failure Immediately After Lift-Off:** Maintain directional control, lower the nose immediately to establish a safe Glide attitude, and land straight ahead on the remaining runway or overrun area.
* **Engine Failure at Low Altitude:** Never attempt to turn back to the runway ("the impossible turn") unless sufficient altitude and performance have been established. Plan ahead for suitable off-field landing sites aligned within a narrow sector ahead of the aircraft.

### Wake Turbulence Avoidance

Departing behind large or heavy transport aircraft presents severe wake turbulence hazards. Wingtip vortices generate intense downwash and roll rates that can exceed an airplane’s control authority. 

When departing behind a larger aircraft, note its rotation point. Plan to rotate **prior** to the preceding aircraft’s rotation point and maintain a climb path that remains above and upwind of its flightpath.

## Practical Application: The Normal Takeoff

A normal takeoff utilizes standard procedures on a firm, smooth runway aligned closely with the existing surface wind.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p136_3.png" alt="Alignment and scan on the runway centerline during normal takeoff" />

### Takeoff Roll Execution

1. **Runway Alignment:** Taxi onto the runway centerline and align the nose-wheel precisely parallel to the runway direction. Pick distant visual reference points (such as runway centerline markings, distant terrain, or towers) to help maintain directional Heading during the acceleration phase.
2. **Brake Release and Power Application:** Release the wheel brakes completely. Advance the throttle smoothly and continuously to full takeoff power. Avoid abrupt throttle movements, which induce severe left-turning tendencies.
3. **Foot Placement:** Keep the balls or toes of the feet centered on the rudder pedals, keeping the heels flat on the floor to avoid inadvertent brake application during the ground roll.
4. **Instrument Crosscheck:** Glance quickly at the engine gauges (oil pressure, RPM/manifold pressure) and the airspeed indicator to confirm the engine is producing full power and airspeed is actively rising.
5. **Directional Control:** As the airplane accelerates, left-turning tendencies (engine torque, P-factor, gyroscopic precession, and spiraling slipstream) become pronounced. Apply smooth, positive right rudder pressure as required to keep the aircraft tracking down the centerline. 

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p139_4.png" alt="Rudder control application and aerodynamic forces during takeoff roll" />

> **Attention:** During the takeoff roll in tricycle-gear aircraft, the elevator should remain relaxed or neutral. Applying premature back-pressure increases aerodynamic Drag and delays acceleration, while forward pressure places unnecessary load on the nose landing gear.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p140_5.png" alt="Elevator control position and nosewheel loading during the takeoff ground roll" />

### Lift-Off and Pitch Rotation

As the airplane approaches the manufacturer's recommended rotation speed ($V_R$):

1. **Smooth Back-Pressure:** Apply steady, smooth elevator back-pressure to rotate the aircraft into the target climb attitude.
2. **Attitude Control:** Allow the nose to rise smoothly to the proper Pitch attitude. Do not pull the airplane off the runway abruptly, as an excessively high Angle of Attack creates induced Drag, prolongs the takeoff roll, and risks an aerodynamic Stall close to the ground.
3. **Wings Level:** Keep the wings laterally level using coordinated aileron inputs. The main wheels should separate cleanly from the runway surface without settling back down.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p141_6.png" alt="Lift-off rotation and establishing the proper pitch attitude" />

### Initial Climb Phase

Once airborne:

1. **Establish Climb Airspeed:** Maintain the climb Pitch attitude that yields the recommended best rate-of-climb speed ($V_Y$) or best angle-of-climb speed ($V_X$) as required.
2. **Directional Tracking:** Keep the nose tracking straight along the extended runway centerline using outside visual references and coordinated rudder pressure. Compensate for any Crosswind drift by establishing a coordinated crab angle into the wind.
3. **Trim Adjustment:** Once the climb attitude and airspeed stabilize, relieve control pressures by adjusting elevator Trim.
4. **Safe Maneuvering Altitude:** Maintain full climb power and the designated climb speed until reaching a safe maneuvering altitude (typically 500 to 1,000 feet above ground level) before making turns or lowering the pitch attitude to an en route climb configuration.

## Common Errors and Safety Summary

Executing safe takeoffs requires continuous discipline. The following common errors frequently lead to incidents or checkride unsatisfactory performance:

* Failing to calculate takeoff performance based on local Density Altitude.
* Applying takeoff power abruptly, resulting in loss of directional control from engine torque and P-factor.
* Resting toes on the brake pedals during acceleration, causing tire scrubbing or directional instability.
* Over-rotating or pulling the airplane into the air prematurely, inducing high Drag or an aerodynamic Stall.
* Failing to maintain extended runway centerline alignment during the initial departure climb.
* Neglecting to brief emergency procedures prior to entering the active runway.
