## Introduction

Approximately twenty percent of all yearly general aviation (GA) accidents occur during the takeoff and departure climb phases of flight. More than half of these occurrences stem from pilot error, with a significant number involving loss of control. Although takeoff occupies a very small portion of total flight duration, pilot workload is concentrated and intense.

This chapter details the knowledge, procedures, and techniques required to perform safe takeoffs and departure climbs in tricycle-gear airplanes under normal and challenging operational environments.

> **Attention:** Always consult the FAA-approved Airplane Flight Manual or Pilot’s Operating Handbook (AFM/POH) for your specific make and model. If procedures or airspeeds in this chapter differ from the manufacturer's approved materials, the AFM/POH takes precedence.

## Phases of the Takeoff Maneuver

Although a takeoff and climb is experienced as one smooth, continuous maneuver, it is broken down into three distinct operational steps for analysis and technique:

1. **Takeoff Roll (Ground Roll):** The portion of the maneuver where the airplane accelerates from a standstill to an airspeed providing sufficient Lift to become airborne.
2. **Lift-off (Rotation):** The transition point where the wings support the full Weight of the airplane. This is achieved by smoothly rotating the nose up to increase the Angle of Attack (AOA).
3. **Initial Climb:** Begins when the wheels leave the surface and a climb pitch attitude is established. It concludes when the airplane attains a safe maneuvering altitude or transitions into an en route climb configuration.

## Pre-Takeoff Planning and Preparation

Safe departures begin before reaching the active runway. Reviewing performance charts ensures the airplane can safely clear obstacles and accelerate within the available runway length under prevailing conditions.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p134_2.png" alt="Performance chart examples showing take-off distance and rate of climb versus density altitude" />

### Atmospheric Considerations
High Density Altitude severely degrades engine power, propeller efficiency, and aerodynamic Lift. This combination increases ground roll distance and decreases rate of climb. Pilots must calculate takeoff distances based on Pressure Altitude and ambient temperature rather than indicated airfield elevation alone.

### Run-Up and Emergency Briefing
Prior to entering the runway:
- Complete all pre-takeoff checks and verify engine instruments show normal indications.
- Verify flight controls are free, correct, and unrestricted throughout their full range of motion.
- Conduct a pre-takeoff briefing detailing abort criteria while on the runway and emergency actions in the event of an engine failure after lift-off (including planned landing sites, wind direction, and avoidance of aggressive turns back to the runway at low altitude).
- Clear the final approach path and runway environment for traffic. At non-towered airports, announce intentions on the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF); at towered fields, obtain explicit takeoff clearance.

> **Attention:** When departing behind large or heavy transport aircraft, plan to avoid wake turbulence encounters. Rotate prior to the preceding aircraft's rotation point and climb above its flightpath, or observe recommended wake separation intervals.

## Normal Takeoff Technique

A normal takeoff is performed into the wind whenever possible. Aligning the takeoff heading with a Headwind yields two distinct advantages:
1. **Existing Airspeed:** The headwind provides initial Indicated Airspeed (IAS) before ground roll begins.
2. **Reduced Groundspeed:** A lower Groundspeed is required to attain flying speed, producing a shorter ground roll and reducing wear and stress on tires and landing gear.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p136_3.png" alt="Runway alignment and reference point tracking during takeoff roll" />

### Takeoff Roll Execution
1. Steer the airplane onto the runway centerline using rudder pedals, aligning the nose-wheel with the runway heading.
2. Select distant visual ground references aligned with the runway centerline (e.g., runway markings, distant terrain features, or structures) to maintain directional awareness.
3. Smoothly and continuously advance the throttle to full takeoff power. Avoid abrupt throttle movements that create sharp left-turning Yaw from engine torque, P-factor, and gyroscopic precession.
4. Position feet with the balls of the feet resting on the rudder pedals rather than the brake pedals to prevent inadvertent braking.
5. Apply appropriate right rudder pressure to counter left-turning tendencies and keep the airplane tracking straight along the centerline.
6. Maintain neutral elevator control until reaching rotation speed ($V_R$), applying only enough back-pressure to steady the yoke or stick.

## Lift-off and Initial Climb

As the airplane accelerates to rotation speed ($V_R$), smoothly apply back-elevator pressure to establish the pitch attitude that produces the manufacturer's recommended climb airspeed.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p140_5.png" alt="Lift-off pitch attitude adjustment and initial climb transition" />

### Lift-Off
- Avoid over-rotating, which causes an excessive Angle of Attack, high induced Drag, and potential premature aerodynamic Stall.
- Once airborne, allow the airplane to accelerate smoothly to the appropriate climb speed:
  - **$V_X$ (Best Angle of Climb):** Provides the greatest gain in altitude over a given horizontal distance; used for clearing obstacles.
  - **$V_Y$ (Best Rate of Climb):** Delivers the greatest gain in altitude over a given period of time; used for standard departures.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p141_6.png" alt="Airspeed management and pitch coordination in the climb" />

### Climb-Out and Trim
- Cross-check engine instruments, verify positive rate of climb, and maintain runway centerline alignment visually.
- When clear of obstacles and at a safe altitude (typically 500 to 1,000 feet above ground level), establish an en route climb speed if recommended by the POH to improve forward visibility and engine cooling.
- Apply elevator Trim to relieve control pressures once the climb pitch attitude and desired airspeed have stabilized.

<img src="/images/AFH/ch06/img_p142_7.png" alt="Transition from initial climb to en route climb attitude" />

> **Attention:** Trimming during rapid speed transitions or prior to establishing an attitude forces the pilot to fight shifting control pressures. Establish the target pitch attitude first, verify the stabilized airspeed, and then trim out the control force.

## Common Takeoff Errors

To achieve consistent proficiency and safety, pilots must identify and avoid common operational mistakes during departure:

- **Improper Throttle Application:** Abrupt advancement leads to directional control difficulties; hesitant or partial advancement extends ground roll unnecessarily.
- **Riding the Brakes:** Inadvertent brake application during the ground roll causes asymmetric drag, extends takeoff roll, and leads to excessive brake wear or tire blowout.
- **Over-Rotation:** Abruptly pulling the nose up at lift-off can result in high drag, settled flight back to the runway, or a departure stall close to the ground.
- **Failure to Maintain Centerline:** Inadequate rudder input to counteract torque, P-factor, and crosswind drift allows the airplane to drift off runway heading.
- **Fixating Inside the Cockpit:** Focusing strictly on airspeed or engine gauges degrades directional control; divide attention between outside references and instrument cross-checks.

---

## Summary

The takeoff and departure climb demands precise control coordination, thorough pre-flight performance calculations, and proactive threat management. Dividing the maneuver into ground roll, lift-off, and initial climb enables student pilots to systematically build the motor skills and situational awareness necessary for safe departures under all operating conditions.
